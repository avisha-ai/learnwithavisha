Because the temperature driving force (ΔT) remains high along the entire exchanger length. In parallel flow, both streams move in the same direction — hot fluid cools while cold fluid warms, so their temperatures converge near the exit and ΔT collapses. Countercurrent keeps the hot inlet opposite the cold outlet, maintaining a useful gradient at every cross-section. The result: the same duty with significantly less heat transfer area. That is why nearly every industrial shell-and-tube runs countercurrent.→ See Fig. 1 above · EP 01

LMTD — Log Mean Temperature Difference — is the correct average ΔT to use in Q = U·A·LMTD. You need it because ΔT changes continuously along the exchanger. A simple arithmetic average overstates performance. LMTD = (ΔT₁ − ΔT₂) / ln(ΔT₁/ΔT₂), where ΔT₁ and ΔT₂ are the temperature differences at each end. For anything other than pure counterflow, apply a geometry correction factor F.

A PID controller outputs a correction signal equal to P·e + I·∫e dt + D·de/dt, where e is the error — setpoint minus measurement. Proportional action (P) responds immediately but leaves a residual offset at steady state. Integral action (I) eliminates that offset by accumulating error over time, though it can cause overshoot. Derivative action (D) anticipates rate of change, damping the response before overshoot occurs. Most plant loops run as PI; derivative is added selectively where sensor noise is manageable.

Cavitation occurs when local pressure at the pump suction falls below the fluid's vapour pressure. Liquid flashes to vapour, forming bubbles that collapse violently as pressure recovers inside the impeller. High suction lift increases the static head the pump must overcome to draw fluid — lowering suction pressure further. The fix: reduce friction losses in the suction line, lower pump elevation relative to the liquid source, raise the liquid level, or reduce fluid temperature to lower vapour pressure.
