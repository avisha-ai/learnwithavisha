EPISODE 02 · HEAT TRANSFER · NOW SHOWING

“Designed clean. Six months later, less heat.
Where did the extra resistance come from?”

A deposit just 0.08 mm thick adds a resistance in series with the tube wall and the two films. U falls about 17%, the outlets drift, and the exchanger moves about 6% less heat.

Episode 02 · The Heat Exchanger Files
Read the transcript

Your heat exchanger was designed clean. Six months later, it's struggling. Nobody touched it. What happened? Same exchanger. Same flows. Hot in at 90, cold in at 10 °C. Clean, the cold stream left at 63. Now it leaves at 60. What changed is a layer inside the tubes, 0.08 mm thick. Thinner than a sheet of paper. It doesn't block the flow. It sits in the path of the heat. Resistances in series simply add. Clean, the resistance to heat flow is 0.0020 m²·K/W. The deposit adds 0.0004. So U falls from 500 to about 417 W/m²·K. That sliver resists heat like about 6.4 mm of stainless steel. Lower U means fewer transfer units: 2 becomes about 1.7. Effectiveness slips from 0.67 to 0.63, and the exchanger moves about 6% less heat. 6% is easy to miss. Flow is normal. The only clue is a drifting thermometer. Fouling is a resistance in series. Watch the temperatures, not just the flow. These numbers are an illustrative worked example. Next: why are there plates inside the shell?
